03 — Reviews

Collecting reviews patients can actually trust

Reviews are the first trust signal for Italian patients, but Doctolib only showed five Google reviews on a fraction of profiles. I designed how we collect our own verified reviews after each appointment, shaped by what patients told us makes a review useful.

Part 1 · Context

Why Google reviews weren’t enough

The problem

Italian patients rely on reviews to choose a practitioner they’ve never met. Since June 2026, Doctolib displayed Google reviews on Italian profiles. It made no significant difference to bookings.

Limit 01

Low coverage

We could only match half of our profiles with Google, and ended up showing reviews on about 2,500 of them.

Limit 02

Five reviews, no nuance

Google only shares five reviews, mostly 5 stars or 1 star. Only 5% of patients clicked to see more.

Limit 03

Not verified

Nothing told patients the reviewer had actually seen the practitioner, and every Italian competitor runs its own verified reviews.

Screen to add: the Google reviews section on an Italian profile

What we already had

Doctolib already sent a satisfaction survey after appointments, but it was anonymous, had no free text, ran on a legacy design and was answered by around 7% of patients, mostly happy ones.

Volume was the real challenge. Asking only patients who booked online, it would take a year to reach 12 reviews for half of our practitioners. The flow had to reach patients booked offline too, who represent most appointments in Italy.

Part 2 · Research

What makes a review worth reading

We ran a survey on Screeb and an unmoderated card sorting on Maze with Italian patients, to understand how they read reviews before choosing a new practitioner.

Three questions, three answers

How they choose

The person over the place

“Listens with empathy” (93.5% top 3) and “explains clearly” (85.5%) far outrank equipment, punctuality or a welcoming practice.

→ Ask about the human side first.
What makes a review useful

Proof it’s real

A verified visit (67.3% top 3) and a verified profile (55.1%) come first, then detail and recency. Photos come last (4.1%).

→ Only ask patients who attended.
How they read them

Simple beats clever

Rating distribution (68.2%) and sorting by rating or date are used most. Keyword filters and AI summaries come last.

→ Collect structured ratings to display later.

Part 3 · Design

A one-minute review, right after the appointment

The right moment, the right patient

At 8pm on the day of the appointment, patients receive an email and a push notification: “How was your appointment? It only takes a minute.” The link opens the form in the Doctolib app if installed, or in the browser, without logging in.

  • Only real visits: no-shows, cancellations, teleconsultations and appointments booked for a relative are excluded, so every review comes from the person who was there.
  • Online and offline bookings, to reach the volume we need.
  • No fatigue: one request per practitioner per year, 14 days to answer, and it replaces the old survey instead of adding a second email.
Screens to add: email and push notification

Zoom: the review form

The form opens with a summary of the appointment (practitioner, date, place and visit reason) so patients know exactly what they’re reviewing. The questions mirror what patients said matters to them:

QuestionWhy
Would you recommend this practitioner? RequiredA recommendation from relatives is the ultimate trust signal
Warmth and attentivenessPatients’ #1 criterion
Clarity of explanationsPatients’ #2 criterion
Equipment · Waiting timeSecondary criteria, still useful context
Share your experience OptionalDetailed reviews rank high, with guidance to keep medical details out

Sub-ratings bring the nuance Google reviews lacked: a patient can love the practitioner and still find the wait too long. Only the recommendation and the publication consent are required, so the form stays quick.

Bringing practitioners along

Admins can switch the review email on or off in their settings, and practitioners are told 15 days before launch. Next, they’ll see their reviews aggregated into scores for each question, so they understand the value instead of switching it off.

Status In progress

What I learned

  • Collection is a design problem too. What you ask, and when, decides what you’ll be able to show later.
  • Trust comes from proof. Patients care less about how many reviews there are than about knowing they’re real.
  • Design for both sides. Without practitioners on board, there are no reviews to show.
