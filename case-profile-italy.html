Designing the profile page around how Italian patients choose

In Italy, profile visits turned into bookings four times less often than in France. I reworked the practitioner profile around the order in which Italian patients choose a doctor, and tested each change on its own. Adding decision content lifted bookings by up to +5.1%; moving existing content around did not.

Part 1 · Discovery

Understanding why Italy doesn't convert

The problem

The practitioner profile is where a patient decides whether to book. In Italy, that decision went the wrong way far more often than in our mature markets: 6% of profile visits led to a booking, against 27% in France and 19% in Germany.

The gap held even in well-supplied cities such as Milan, which ruled out a lack of practitioners. The problem was the page itself.

Screen to add: the Italian mobile profile before the redesign

What the data told us

Most of the information patients needed already existed on the page. They just never saw it.

Insight 01

Content hidden behind “See more”

Expand buttons sat in the top-right corner of each card, with content collapsed by default. Only 3% of patients opened the location section.

→ Show by default, expand from the bottom.
Insight 02

Half the page is never seen

50% of patients didn't scroll past the fees section. Everything below mid-page reached at most half of visitors.

→ Bring key information above the fold.
Insight 03

Search engine visitors leave

30% of patients arriving from Google left without any action. Many went back to their results instead of exploring Doctolib.

→ Give them a reason to keep looking here.

What patients told us

Ten interviews with Italian patients revealed a clear order in how they choose a practitioner. It became the backbone of the whole project.

Diagram to add: Location → Trust → Expertise → Price → Availability

Location comes first, then trust, where reviews and ratings are the strongest signal. Patients dropped profiles when a practitioner's expertise was unclear.

What the market does

I benchmarked the main Italian and international booking platforms. They follow the same conventions, which the Doctolib profile didn't:

  • Key information at the top of the page
  • Tab navigation to reach details further down
  • A sticky booking button while scrolling
  • Expand actions at the bottom of each section

The foundation: making content discoverable

Before adding anything, I made existing content findable. Every expand button moved to the bottom of its card and now says what it opens (“See all 6 trainings” rather than “See more”). Location and accessibility merged into one card. Opening hours, which had the highest “See more” click rate, are now fully visible.

This layout became the base every other change plugs into.

Zoom: the profile hero

On mobile, the top of the profile only showed a name, a photo, a specialty and sometimes a rating. Patients had to scroll the whole page to learn anything else.

The new hero adds up to three rows right below the name: rating and review count (plus years of experience), the first visit reason and its price, and expertise, or the team size for an establishment. Each row only shows when the data exists, so there are no empty states. Tapping a row opens the same detail as the matching section further down.

A sticky, swipeable navigation bar under the hero lists only the sections this profile actually has.

Zoom: a map you can actually see

Location is the first thing Italian patients look at, yet the map sat behind a “See more” that 3% of them opened. The map now shows on page load, with the “See itinerary” button next to it.

Map consent remains mandatory: patients who haven't consented see a short prompt in the map area. In interviews, Italian patients accepted it readily when the value was clear.

Spoiler: this is the change that taught us the most. See the results below.

Part 3 · Delivery

Designed to be tested, one change at a time

Rather than ship one big redesign, I split the work into six independent changes, each behind its own A/B test. That way we learn what each change is worth instead of guessing which part moved the number.

  • Italy only: France and Germany stay unchanged, and any change can be switched off on its own.
  • One documented dependency: the anchor navigation relies on the new hero, so the hero ships and is validated first.
  • Guard rails: showing more content by default is monitored against page load time.

In parallel, the team made the profile page more modular so we could iterate on it faster.

What the results taught us

Learning 01

Content, not layout

Re-arranging what we already showed never moved bookings. Adding information that helps people decide, like expertise, did.

Learning 02

What people say ≠ what they do

Patients ranked location first in interviews, yet showing the map more prominently lowered bookings for paid specialists. Proximity still matters, as an input to search rather than as a display.

Learning 03

Fewer steps win

Only changes that bring many more patients into the booking flow move bookings. The sticky button did exactly that.

Most patients land from Google and don’t know the practitioner yet. They need trust and social proof to choose, more than navigation. These learnings now feed Doctolib’s search vision for 2027.

What I learned

  • Test what people say against what they do. Research told us where to look; A/B tests told us what actually helps patients book.
  • Often the content is already there. Making it reachable paid off; reshuffling it didn’t.
  • Split to learn. One test per change made every result readable, including the ones that failed.
